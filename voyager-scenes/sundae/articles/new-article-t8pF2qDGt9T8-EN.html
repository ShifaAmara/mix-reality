The history of the ice cream sundae is a delicious, yet hotly contested, piece of American culinary folklore. With no single, universally accepted origin story, several cities across the United States lay claim to inventing the iconic dessert. The most prominent rivalries are between Ithaca, New York, and Two Rivers, Wisconsin, with other towns citing their own unique contributions.

The common thread in the sundae's origin is its connection to Sunday. The dessert was often created as a response to or in defiance of "blue laws"—religious laws that forbade the sale of certain items, like ice cream sodas, on the Sabbath. To circumvent these restrictions, soda fountain owners would serve ice cream with syrup but without the carbonated water, creating a "soda-less" treat.

Here are the leading claims to the invention of the ice cream sundae:


Two Rivers, Wisconsin (1881)
 

The town of Two Rivers presents one of the earliest claims. According to local legend, on a Sunday in 1881, customer George Hallauer asked Edward C. Berner, the owner of a soda fountain and confectionery, to top a dish of ice cream with the chocolate syrup typically used for making ice cream sodas.

Berner was initially hesitant, believing the syrup should only be used for sodas, but eventually complied. The new creation was an instant hit. Berner began selling the "ice cream with syrup" for a nickel, but only on Sundays. The story goes that a young girl once insisted on having one on a weekday, asking to "pretend it was Sunday." Soon after, Berner was selling the treat every day. The name "sundae" is said to have been coined when a glassware salesman began marketing the canoe-shaped dishes used to serve it as "sundae dishes."


Ithaca, New York (1892)
 

Ithaca's claim is distinguished by strong historical documentation. On Sunday, April 3, 1892, Reverend John M. Scott of the local Unitarian Church visited the Platt & Colt Pharmacy. The pharmacy's proprietor, Chester C. Platt, who was also the church treasurer, decided to prepare a special treat. He put two scoops of vanilla ice cream in a dish and, on a whim, topped them with cherry syrup and a candied cherry.

The Reverend and Platt were so taken with the dessert that they decided it needed a name. They named it "Cherry Sunday" in honor of the day it was created. Crucially, the Platt & Colt Pharmacy placed an advertisement in the Ithaca Daily Journal on April 5, 1892, for their new "Cherry Sunday." This ad serves as the earliest known physical evidence of a dessert named for the day.


Evanston, Illinois (c. 1890)
 

Evanston's story is directly tied to the blue laws. As a strict Methodist town, Evanston passed an ordinance in 1890 that prohibited the sale of ice cream sodas on Sundays. To avoid losing business, local soda fountain owners began serving ice cream topped with syrup, effectively a soda without the soda. This creation was often referred to as a "Sunday Soda." However, to avoid offending the religious community by naming a dessert after the Sabbath, the spelling was changed to "sundae."


The Spelling: From "Sunday" to "Sundae"
 

Regardless of its exact origin, the change in spelling from "Sunday" to "sundae" is a consistent theme across most narratives. The primary reason cited is a desire to be more secular and avoid accusations of blasphemy from religious leaders who objected to a commercial treat being named after the holy day. Another practical theory is that it helped to distinguish the dessert from the day of the week, allowing it to be marketed and sold every day.

While the debate over its true birthplace may never be settled, the ice cream sundae quickly became an American institution by the early 20th century, evolving from a simple scoop with syrup to the elaborate, multi-topping dessert beloved today.
